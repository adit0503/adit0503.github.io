Fine-Tuning & PEFT — LoRA, When & How

The decision framework for fine-tuning vs RAG vs prompting, the spectrum from full fine-tuning to parameter-efficient methods like LoRA and QLoRA, instruction tuning and preference alignment, dataset preparation, and how to evaluate whether it actually helped.

When to Fine-Tune

The most common interview trap is reaching for fine-tuning to add knowledge. Fine-tuning teaches behavior, style, and format — not facts. Facts belong in RAG.

  • Fine-tune for — a consistent tone/persona, a strict output format, a narrow classification/extraction task, domain jargon, or reducing prompt length by baking in instructions.
  • Use RAG for — knowledge that changes, must be cited, or is access-controlled.
  • Use prompting for — anything a good prompt with few-shot examples already handles (try this first).
🔑 The ladder:

Prompt engineering → RAG → fine-tuning, in that order of effort. Exhaust the cheaper rungs first. Fine-tuning is the last resort when prompting and retrieval have plateaued on a behavior the model consistently gets wrong.

The Fine-Tuning Spectrum

  • Full fine-tuning — update all weights. Maximum adaptation, but expensive, needs lots of data and GPUs, and produces a full-size model copy per task.
  • Parameter-efficient (PEFT) — freeze the base model, train a tiny set of new parameters. Cheap, fast, small artifacts. The default for most teams.
  • Continued pretraining — more unsupervised training on domain text to shift the base distribution (rarely needed outside heavily specialized domains).

PEFT — LoRA & QLoRA

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) freezes the original weights and injects small trainable low-rank matrices into the model's layers. You train only those — often <1% of parameters — yet capture most of the benefit of full fine-tuning.

  • Tiny artifacts — a LoRA adapter is megabytes, not gigabytes; you can host many adapters over one base model.
  • Key hyperparameters — rank r (capacity), alpha (scaling), and which layers to target.
  • QLoRA — quantize the frozen base model to 4-bit and train LoRA adapters on top, so fine-tuning fits on a single GPU. Democratized fine-tuning.
  • Adapter swapping — serve one base model and hot-swap task-specific adapters at inference.
💡 Start with LoRA:

For nearly all applied work, LoRA/QLoRA gets you most of full fine-tuning's quality at a fraction of the cost and operational complexity. Only consider full fine-tuning if LoRA provably falls short.

Instruction Tuning & Alignment

  • Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) — train on curated (instruction, ideal response) pairs. This is what most applied fine-tuning means.
  • Preference alignment (RLHF / DPO) — tune toward preferred responses using pairs of better/worse outputs. DPO is simpler than full RLHF and increasingly the default for preference tuning.
  • Distillation — fine-tune a small model on a large model's outputs to get most of the quality at lower cost/latency.

Dataset Preparation

Fine-tuning is a data problem, not a modeling problem. Quality and consistency beat volume.

  • Format consistency — every example must match the exact prompt/response structure you'll use in production.
  • Quality over quantity — a few hundred to a few thousand clean, correct examples usually outperform a huge noisy set.
  • Coverage — include edge cases and the failure modes you're trying to fix.
  • Deduplicate & split — remove near-duplicates; hold out a validation and test set that never leak into training.
  • Synthetic data — a stronger model can generate/expand examples, but review for quality and bias.
⚠️ Catastrophic forgetting:

Aggressive fine-tuning on a narrow task can degrade the model's general abilities. Use conservative learning rates, PEFT (which freezes the base), and evaluate on general tasks too — not just your target task.

Evaluation & Pitfalls

  • Compare against baselines — the base model with good prompting and with RAG. Fine-tuning must beat those to justify itself.
  • Held-out test set — measure on data the model never saw; watch for overfitting to the training style.
  • Regression check — confirm general capability didn't drop.
  • Total cost — training + hosting + the ops burden of maintaining a custom model and re-tuning as needs change.
  • Managed options — Bedrock and Databricks both offer managed fine-tuning so you skip GPU infrastructure.

Interview Questions

RAG or fine-tuning to make a model answer from company docs?

RAG. Fine-tuning teaches behavior, style, and format — not facts — and baked-in knowledge goes stale, can't be cited, and can't respect per-user access. RAG retrieves current, attributable, access-controlled context at query time and updates instantly. Fine-tuning would complement RAG only to fix how the model responds (tone, format), while RAG supplies what it responds with.

Explain LoRA and why it's so widely used.

LoRA freezes the pretrained weights and inserts small trainable low-rank matrices into the layers, so you train under ~1% of parameters while capturing most of full fine-tuning's benefit. The adapters are tiny (megabytes), training is cheap and fast, you avoid a full model copy per task, and you can hot-swap adapters over one base model at serving time. QLoRA extends this by quantizing the base to 4-bit so it fits on a single GPU.

What is catastrophic forgetting and how do you avoid it?

It's when fine-tuning on a narrow task erodes the model's general capabilities. Mitigate with parameter-efficient methods like LoRA that freeze the base weights, conservative learning rates, fewer epochs, and mixing in some general-purpose examples. Crucially, evaluate on general benchmarks alongside your target task so you catch regressions rather than only measuring the narrow win.

How much data do you need, and what matters most?

Quality and consistency far outweigh volume — often a few hundred to a few thousand clean, correctly formatted examples beat a huge noisy dataset. Every example must match the production prompt/response format, cover real edge cases and target failure modes, be deduplicated, and be split so the test set never leaks into training. Synthetic data from a stronger model can help but needs review for quality and bias.

How do you know fine-tuning was worth it?

Beat the right baselines on a held-out test set: the base model with strong prompting and few-shot, and the RAG setup. If fine-tuning doesn't clearly outperform those, it's not justified given its training, hosting, and maintenance cost. Also verify general capability didn't regress and factor in the ongoing burden of re-tuning as requirements evolve — a static fine-tune can't track changing knowledge the way RAG does.
