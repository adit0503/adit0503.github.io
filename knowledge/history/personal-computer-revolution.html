The Personal Computer Revolution

The microprocessor put a CPU on a chip; the personal computer put that chip on every desk. This is the era of hobbyist kits, garage startups, the graphical interface, and the software companies that came to dominate the industry.

The microprocessor had put the brain of a computer onto a chip cheap enough for anyone to buy. But an industry built around giant corporate mainframes could not imagine why an individual would want a computer, any more than a person would want their own power station. The revolution of the 1970s and 80s was therefore as much cultural as technical: a shift in imagination, led at first not by companies but by hobbyists, dreamers, and a scruffy counterculture of tinkerers who simply wanted a machine of their own. This chapter follows how the computer left the corporate machine room and landed on the desk — and how the fortunes of the whole industry were decided by a handful of pivotal decisions.

The Altair 8800 & the Hobbyists

The spark landed in January 1975, on the cover of a hobbyist electronics magazine. There sat the Altair 8800, a boxy machine built around Intel's new microprocessor by a small New Mexico outfit called MITS, run by an engineer named Ed Roberts. It arrived as a kit you soldered together yourself, had no screen or keyboard, and was "programmed" by flipping rows of switches on its front panel while lights blinked back the answer in binary. By any practical measure it did almost nothing useful.

And yet it electrified a generation. For the first time an ordinary person could own a computer, and orders flooded in far beyond anything MITS had imagined. In Silicon Valley, enthralled hobbyists began gathering to swap ideas and show off their machines at the Homebrew Computer Club — a gathering whose membership rolls would soon read like a founders' list of the entire personal computer industry.

Gates & Allen — Microsoft

Two of the people galvanized by that magazine cover were a young programmer named Paul Allen and his even younger, intensely driven friend Bill Gates. They saw at once what the Altair lacked: a real way to program it. Racing to write a version of the BASIC language for a machine they did not even own — testing it on a simulation and getting it working only on the flight to demonstrate it — they made it a success, and in 1975 founded a company to sell it. They called it Microsoft.

Gates grasped, earlier and more ruthlessly than almost anyone, a truth the hobbyist culture resisted: that software itself was the valuable product, worth paying for, not something to be freely copied among friends. His pointed 1976 "Open Letter to Hobbyists," scolding those who pirated his BASIC, drew a line that would define the industry. But the decision that made Microsoft an empire came in 1980, and its full weight belongs to the story of IBM below — for it turned on the difference between selling something and merely licensing it.

Gary Kildall — CP/M

Before Microsoft dominated, the reigning operating system for these early microcomputers was CP/M, written by a talented and easygoing computer scientist named Gary Kildall. His work was genuinely pioneering — he devised the idea of a BIOS, a layer of software that hid the differences between machines so the same programs could run on many brands of hardware, an abstraction still with us today. For a time, CP/M was the software platform of the microcomputer world.

Then came one of the most fateful missed opportunities in business history. When IBM went looking for an operating system for its forthcoming personal computer, it approached Kildall first. Exactly what happened is still debated — a flight taken, a meeting missed, a contract's terms disputed — but the deal fell through, and IBM turned instead to Microsoft. In that single broken negotiation, the future of the industry pivoted from Kildall to Gates.

Jobs & Wozniak — Apple

Out of the Homebrew Computer Club came the era's most storied partnership. Steve Wozniak was a shy engineering genius who designed circuits for the sheer joy of elegance; his friend Steve Jobs was a mesmerizing, demanding visionary who understood that a computer could be not just a tool but an object of desire. Wozniak designed; Jobs saw what it could become and how to sell it. In 1976 they founded Apple, famously in a garage.

Wozniak's Apple II, released in 1977, was a breakthrough: not a kit but a friendly, fully assembled computer with color graphics that an ordinary family could bring home and switch on. It became one of the first genuinely mass-market personal computers and made the pair wealthy. But Jobs was already looking further ahead — toward a machine that would not merely be usable, but effortless. The seeds of that ambition lay in what he would see on a now-legendary visit to a research center across the valley.

Xerox PARC & the GUI

That research center was Xerox PARC, and its story is the great cautionary tale of the industry. In the 1970s the copier giant Xerox funded a laboratory of brilliant minds and left them free to invent the future — and invent it they did, almost the entire modern computing experience, decades before the world was ready. At PARC they built the graphical user interface: windows, icons, and menus that you manipulated by pointing and clicking with a mouse, replacing cryptic typed commands with a screen you could simply see and touch. They created the Alto, the first computer designed around this idea, along with Ethernet networking, the laser printer, and much else. (The mouse and the very idea of interactive, linked computing had been foreshadowed even earlier by Douglas Engelbart, whose 1968 demonstration was so far ahead of its time it is remembered as the "Mother of All Demos.")

Xerox, a company that sold copiers, had little idea what it possessed. When Steve Jobs toured PARC in 1979, he reportedly could barely contain himself — he saw the future glowing on those screens, and Xerox had not thought to lock the door. The graphical interface would go on to make Apple's fortune and, later, Microsoft's; Xerox captured almost none of the value of what its own people had created.

⚠️ Invention is not the same as commercialization:

PARC's fate is the enduring lesson of this chapter. Having a revolutionary idea is not enough; a company must also be willing and able to bring it to market. Xerox invented the modern desktop and let it walk out the door. Apple and Microsoft, who understood how to sell it, reaped the rewards.

The IBM PC & the Standard

For years the personal computer had been the province of hobbyists and upstarts, dismissed by the corporate world. That changed in 1981 when IBM, the towering giant of business computing, entered the fray with the IBM PC. Its arrival instantly made the personal computer respectable — if Big Blue was selling one, every company could justify buying one.

But IBM, moving fast, made a decision whose consequences it did not foresee. Rather than build everything itself, it assembled the PC from off-the-shelf parts and published its specifications — an open architecture. This let other companies, beginning with Compaq, build machines that were fully compatible "clones," and a vast competitive market of interchangeable PCs exploded around IBM's design. The great irony is that IBM had opened a door through which its own dominance escaped. The real winners were the two suppliers who sat at the choke points every clone maker needed: Intel, which made the processors, and Microsoft, which — thanks to that 1980 deal — licensed the operating system to everyone. IBM had set the standard; Gates and the chipmakers inherited the kingdom.

Key Questions

Why did Microsoft become dominant while CP/M faded?

When IBM needed an OS for its PC, the deal with Kildall's CP/M fell through and IBM licensed MS-DOS from Microsoft. Crucially, Microsoft kept the right to license DOS to the IBM-compatible clone makers, so as the clone market exploded, MS-DOS (and later Windows) rode that standard to ubiquity.

What was significant about Xerox PARC?

PARC invented the GUI, mouse-driven desktop, Ethernet, laser printing, and much of modern personal computing in the 1970s. Xerox didn't commercialize it effectively, but the ideas were absorbed by Apple's Macintosh and later Windows, defining how people interact with computers to this day.

Why did the IBM PC's open architecture matter?

Because IBM used standard components and published specs, other companies could legally build compatible "clones." This created a huge competitive hardware market around a single standard, drove prices down, and made the CPU (Intel) and OS (Microsoft) the real centers of value rather than the hardware maker.
