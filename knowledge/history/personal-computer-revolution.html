The Personal Computer Revolution

The microprocessor put a CPU on a chip; the personal computer put that chip on every desk. This is the era of hobbyist kits, garage startups, the graphical interface, and the software companies that came to dominate the industry.

The Altair 8800 & the Hobbyists

The Altair 8800 (1975), built by Ed Roberts at MITS, is widely regarded as the spark of the personal computer revolution.

  • Sold as a kit for hobbyists, programmed with front-panel switches, based on the Intel 8080 microprocessor.
  • It inspired the Homebrew Computer Club in Silicon Valley, whose members included the founders of Apple.

Gates & Allen — Microsoft

Bill Gates and Paul Allen founded Microsoft in 1975.

  • Their first product was Altair BASIC, an interpreter that made the Altair genuinely usable.
  • Their strategy centered on software as the product — licensing, not giving it away. Gates' 1976 "Open Letter to Hobbyists" argued that software deserved to be paid for.
  • In 1980 Microsoft licensed MS-DOS to IBM while retaining the right to sell it to others — the deal that made Microsoft dominant.

Gary Kildall — CP/M

Gary Kildall (1942–1994) created CP/M, the dominant operating system for early microcomputers, and the concept of the BIOS to abstract hardware differences.

  • CP/M was the leading OS before the IBM PC; Kildall is a pioneer of the microcomputer operating system.
  • IBM famously approached him first, but the deal fell through and IBM turned to Microsoft — a pivotal moment that shaped the industry.

Jobs & Wozniak — Apple

Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs founded Apple in 1976.

  • Wozniak engineered the Apple I and the hugely successful Apple II (1977) — one of the first pre-assembled, mass-market personal computers.
  • Jobs provided the vision, design sensibility, and marketing that made computers desirable consumer products.
  • The Macintosh (1984) brought the graphical user interface and mouse to the mass market.

Xerox PARC & the GUI

Many defining ideas of modern computing were invented at Xerox PARC in the 1970s.

  • The graphical user interface (GUI), windows, icons, mouse, WYSIWYG editing, Ethernet, laser printing, and object-oriented Smalltalk.
  • The Xerox Alto was the first computer designed around a GUI.
  • Douglas Engelbart had earlier invented the mouse and demonstrated hypertext and collaboration in the 1968 "Mother of All Demos."
  • Xerox failed to commercialize these ideas; Apple and later Microsoft brought the GUI to the masses.
⚠️ The innovation that got away:

PARC invented much of the modern desktop but Xerox's management didn't capitalize on it. It's a classic cautionary tale about the gap between invention and commercialization.

The IBM PC & the Standard

The IBM PC (1981) legitimized the personal computer for business.

  • IBM used an open architecture with off-the-shelf parts, which allowed clone makers (Compaq and others) to build compatible machines.
  • This created the "IBM-compatible" PC standard that still defines the Windows PC ecosystem, and shifted power to Intel (chips) and Microsoft (OS).

Key Questions

Why did Microsoft become dominant while CP/M faded?

When IBM needed an OS for its PC, the deal with Kildall's CP/M fell through and IBM licensed MS-DOS from Microsoft. Crucially, Microsoft kept the right to license DOS to the IBM-compatible clone makers, so as the clone market exploded, MS-DOS (and later Windows) rode that standard to ubiquity.

What was significant about Xerox PARC?

PARC invented the GUI, mouse-driven desktop, Ethernet, laser printing, and much of modern personal computing in the 1970s. Xerox didn't commercialize it effectively, but the ideas were absorbed by Apple's Macintosh and later Windows, defining how people interact with computers to this day.

Why did the IBM PC's open architecture matter?

Because IBM used standard components and published specs, other companies could legally build compatible "clones." This created a huge competitive hardware market around a single standard, drove prices down, and made the CPU (Intel) and OS (Microsoft) the real centers of value rather than the hardware maker.
