The Electronic Computing Era — ENIAC, Colossus & Hopper

The 1930s–1950s turned theory into hardware. Vacuum tubes replaced gears, wartime pressure accelerated invention, and the first true electronic computers were built — along with the first tools that made them programmable.

The theorists had drawn the map; now the builders set out to explore it. The two decades spanning the 1930s to the 1950s were when computing finally left the drawing board and roared into physical existence — and, as so often in this story, it was war that supplied the money, the urgency, and the pardon for spending fortunes on unproven machines. The great shift of this era was material: the slow, clacking relay and the ghostly, glowing vacuum tube replaced the gear and the lever, and computation became a thing of electricity moving at the speed of thought. What follows is less a tidy succession than a scramble, with several inventors on different continents each building a "first" of some kind, often in ignorance of one another.

Konrad Zuse — The Z3

Our scramble begins, improbably, in a Berlin apartment. Konrad Zuse, a young German civil engineer, was simply tired of the tedious calculations his job demanded, and he set about building a machine to do them — working largely alone, cut off from the developments elsewhere, funding it partly from his own pocket and his parents' living room. In 1941 he completed the Z3, and though the world scarcely noticed at the time, it was the first programmable, fully automatic digital computer ever to work.

Built from some two thousand electromechanical relays, the Z3 read its program from punched film and, remarkably for its day, computed in binary floating-point — the very number format modern processors still use. Zuse even sketched out a high-level programming language he called Plankalkül, decades ahead of its time, though it would go unimplemented for a generation. The Z3 itself did not survive the war; it was obliterated in a 1943 air raid, and Zuse's pioneering work remained obscure for years. A later replica proved the design had been sound all along.

Atanasoff & Berry — The ABC

Across the Atlantic, at Iowa State College, another lone-wolf effort was under way. The physicist John Atanasoff, the story goes, worked out the key principles of his machine during a long, restless drive, sketching ideas on a napkin over a drink. With his graduate student Clifford Berry, he built between 1937 and 1942 the machine that bears their initials — the Atanasoff–Berry Computer, or ABC.

The ABC's distinction is that it was the first computing device to do its arithmetic electronically, using vacuum tubes rather than moving parts, and to work in binary. It was not a general computer — it was built for the single task of solving systems of linear equations, and it could not be programmed — but it proved that electronics could compute. That claim would echo through the courts decades later: in a landmark 1973 ruling, a U.S. judge invalidated the patent on the more famous ENIAC, finding that its inventors had drawn crucial ideas from a visit to Atanasoff. History, it turned out, had underestimated the quiet man from Iowa.

Tommy Flowers — Colossus

Back in Britain, the secret war against German codes was demanding ever more powerful machines. At Bletchley Park the challenge was the Lorenz cipher, used for Hitler's highest-level communications and far more complex than Enigma. A brilliant Post Office telephone engineer named Tommy Flowers proposed a radical answer: a machine built almost entirely from vacuum tubes — fifteen hundred of them and more. His superiors were skeptical, certain so many tubes would fail constantly, so Flowers pressed ahead partly on his own funds and his own conviction.

The result, Colossus (1943–44), was the world's first programmable electronic digital computer, and it worked magnificently, tearing through cipher settings at speeds no human team could approach and feeding vital intelligence to the Allies. Its tragedy is that it was born in secret and buried in secret. After the war most of the Colossus machines were dismantled and the project classified for decades, so it exerted almost no influence on the computers that followed. Flowers received little recognition in his lifetime — a pioneer erased from his own story by the Official Secrets Act.

⚠️ Who built the "first computer"?

You can see why the question has no clean answer. The ABC was first to be electronic but could not be programmed; the Z3 was first to be programmable but used relays, not electronics; Colossus was first to be both electronic and programmable but built for a single purpose. The title most people mean — first electronic, programmable, general-purpose computer — belongs to a machine in Philadelphia. Every "first" depends entirely on which words you emphasize.

Mauchly & Eckert — ENIAC & UNIVAC

That machine was ENIAC, built at the University of Pennsylvania by the physicist John Mauchly and the gifted young engineer J. Presper Eckert, and unveiled in 1945. It was a leviathan: some eighteen thousand vacuum tubes, thirty tons of equipment filling a room, drawing enough power that legend claimed the lights of Philadelphia dimmed when it switched on. Built to compute artillery firing tables for the U.S. Army, it could be turned to almost any numerical problem — the first electronic machine that was truly general-purpose.

But ENIAC carried the flaw von Neumann would soon diagnose: it had no stored program. To give it a new task, its operators had to physically rewire it, plugging and unplugging cables and setting switches across its vast panels — a job that could take days. And here the story rights a long injustice. That painstaking programming was done by a team of six women — Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Holberton, Marlyn Wescoff, Frances Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman — mathematicians who figured out how to make the monster compute and who were, in every real sense, among the world's first software engineers. For decades they went uncredited, mistaken in photographs for models posing beside the machine.

Mauchly and Eckert saw a future beyond the military. They left academia to build a computer for business, and in 1951 delivered UNIVAC I, the first commercial computer in the United States. Its most famous moment came on election night 1952, when, fed early returns, it correctly predicted a landslide for Eisenhower — a result so at odds with expectations that the broadcasters hesitated to air it. When it proved right, the public glimpsed, for the first time, the strange power of the "electronic brain."

Grace Hopper — The Birth of Software

The machines could now compute at electronic speed, but talking to them remained an ordeal of numeric codes intelligible only to specialists. The person who did more than anyone to change that was Grace Hopper, a mathematics professor who joined the U.S. Navy during the war and was assigned to program the room-sized Harvard Mark I. She would remain a force in computing for the rest of her long life, eventually retiring as a rear admiral.

Hopper's revolutionary conviction was that people should not have to write in the machine's language — the machine should meet people halfway. In 1952 she built the first compiler, a program that translated human-readable instructions into the ones and zeros the hardware understood. The idea met resistance; many insisted computers could only crunch numbers and never process something as abstract as language. She proved them wrong, and that single stubborn idea — that software could translate for us — set computing on the road to every programming language that followed. She went on to drive the creation of COBOL, whose English-like syntax opened computing to the business world.

She also left us a piece of everyday vocabulary. When the Mark II computer failed one day in 1947, the culprit turned out to be a literal moth trapped in a relay. Hopper's team taped it into the logbook, noting they had been "debugging" the machine — and though the word predated the moth, her famous logbook entry made it stick.

🔑 The moment software was born:

Before Hopper, programming meant thinking like a machine. After her compiler, the machine did the tedious work of translation, and humans could think in something closer to their own terms. It is the hinge on which the entire history of software turns — the shift from wiring plugboards to writing code.

Key Questions

Why is ENIAC often called the "first computer" when ABC and Colossus came earlier?

ENIAC was the first machine that was simultaneously electronic, general-purpose, and programmable. ABC was electronic but special-purpose and not programmable; Colossus was programmable and electronic but built only for codebreaking. ENIAC could be reprogrammed for arbitrary problems, which is the key qualifier.

What made Grace Hopper's compiler revolutionary?

At the time, many believed computers could only do arithmetic and must be programmed in machine code. Hopper's compiler translated symbolic, human-readable instructions into machine code automatically, proving software abstraction was possible. This idea led directly to high-level languages like COBOL and FORTRAN.

Why were early computers so unreliable?

They used thousands of vacuum tubes, which generated heat and burned out frequently. ENIAC's tubes failed often enough that engineers spent significant time locating dead tubes. This unreliability was a major driver toward the transistor, which was smaller, cooler, and far more dependable.
