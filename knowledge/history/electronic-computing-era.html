The Electronic Computing Era — ENIAC, Colossus & Hopper

The 1930s–1950s turned theory into hardware. Vacuum tubes replaced gears, wartime pressure accelerated invention, and the first true electronic computers were built — along with the first tools that made them programmable.

Konrad Zuse — The Z3

Konrad Zuse (1910–1995), a German engineer, built working computers largely in isolation.

  • His Z3 (1941) was the world's first programmable, fully automatic digital computer, using electromechanical relays and binary floating-point arithmetic.
  • He designed Plankalkül, arguably the first high-level programming language, though it was not implemented for decades.
  • The original Z3 was destroyed in a 1943 air raid; a replica confirmed the design worked.

Atanasoff & Berry — The ABC

John Atanasoff (1903–1995) and his student Clifford Berry built the Atanasoff–Berry Computer (ABC, 1937–1942) at Iowa State College.

  • The first electronic digital computing device to use vacuum tubes for computation and binary representation.
  • Special-purpose (solving systems of linear equations) and not programmable, but pioneering.
  • A 1973 U.S. court ruling (Honeywell v. Sperry Rand) invalidated the ENIAC patent, crediting Atanasoff with key ideas — a landmark in computing history.

Tommy Flowers — Colossus

Tommy Flowers (1905–1998), a British Post Office engineer, built Colossus (1943–1944) at Bletchley Park.

  • The world's first programmable electronic digital computer, using ~1,500–2,400 vacuum tubes.
  • Used to break the German Lorenz cipher (higher-level than Enigma), contributing to Allied victory.
  • Kept secret for decades under wartime classification, so it had little influence on later commercial machines but proved large-scale electronic computing was viable.
⚠️ "First computer" is contested:

ABC (first electronic, not programmable), Z3 (first programmable, electromechanical), Colossus (first programmable electronic, special-purpose), and ENIAC (first general-purpose programmable electronic) each claim a "first" depending on the qualifiers. Context matters.

Mauchly & Eckert — ENIAC & UNIVAC

John Mauchly (1907–1980) and J. Presper Eckert (1919–1995) at the University of Pennsylvania built ENIAC (1945).

  • The first general-purpose, programmable, electronic computer — ~18,000 vacuum tubes, weighing 30 tons.
  • Originally programmed by physically rewiring plugboards; reprogramming took days.
  • The ENIAC programmers — Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Holberton, Marlyn Wescoff, Frances Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman — were pioneering software engineers whose work was long uncredited.
  • Mauchly and Eckert went on to build UNIVAC I (1951), the first commercial computer in the U.S., famous for predicting the 1952 presidential election result on live TV.

Grace Hopper — The Birth of Software

Grace Hopper (1906–1992), a U.S. Navy rear admiral and computer scientist, shaped how humans talk to machines.

  • Programmed the Harvard Mark I during WWII.
  • Built the first compiler (the A-0 system, 1952), proving that programs could be written in human-readable form and translated to machine code.
  • Drove the creation of COBOL (1959), making business computing accessible with English-like syntax.
  • Popularized the term "debugging" after a moth was found in the Mark II relay (the actual moth is taped in her logbook).
🔑 Why it matters:

Hopper's compiler was a radical idea — that a machine should do the tedious work of translation. It set computing on the path from wiring plugboards toward the high-level languages every developer uses today.

Key Questions

Why is ENIAC often called the "first computer" when ABC and Colossus came earlier?

ENIAC was the first machine that was simultaneously electronic, general-purpose, and programmable. ABC was electronic but special-purpose and not programmable; Colossus was programmable and electronic but built only for codebreaking. ENIAC could be reprogrammed for arbitrary problems, which is the key qualifier.

What made Grace Hopper's compiler revolutionary?

At the time, many believed computers could only do arithmetic and must be programmed in machine code. Hopper's compiler translated symbolic, human-readable instructions into machine code automatically, proving software abstraction was possible. This idea led directly to high-level languages like COBOL and FORTRAN.

Why were early computers so unreliable?

They used thousands of vacuum tubes, which generated heat and burned out frequently. ENIAC's tubes failed often enough that engineers spent significant time locating dead tubes. This unreliability was a major driver toward the transistor, which was smaller, cooler, and far more dependable.
