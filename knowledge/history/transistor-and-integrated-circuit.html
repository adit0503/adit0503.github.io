The Transistor & Integrated Circuit Revolution

The single most important hardware advance in computing: replacing the vacuum tube with the transistor, then packing millions of them onto a sliver of silicon. This is how the room-sized computer became the chip in your pocket.

The Transistor — Bardeen, Brattain & Shockley

At Bell Labs in 1947, John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley invented the transistor, a solid-state switch that could amplify and switch electrical signals.

  • It replaced the bulky, hot, fragile vacuum tube with a small, cool, reliable, low-power device.
  • The three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. (Bardeen would later win a second Nobel for superconductivity.)
  • Shockley moved to California and founded Shockley Semiconductor, seeding the region that became Silicon Valley.
🔑 Why it mattered:

The transistor is the fundamental building block of all modern electronics. Every logic gate and memory cell in a computer is built from transistors — a modern CPU contains tens of billions of them.

The Integrated Circuit — Kilby & Noyce

Wiring individual transistors together by hand did not scale. The solution came almost simultaneously from two inventors:

  • Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments (1958) built the first working integrated circuit, placing multiple components on a single piece of semiconductor. He won the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics.
  • Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor (1959) independently devised a silicon-based IC using the planar process, which was far more practical to manufacture — the design that made mass production possible.

Silicon Valley & Fairchild

Eight engineers — the "Traitorous Eight", including Noyce and Gordon Moore — left Shockley Semiconductor in 1957 to found Fairchild Semiconductor.

  • Fairchild became the birthplace of the practical silicon IC and the training ground for a generation of chip pioneers.
  • Its alumni ("Fairchildren") founded dozens of companies, including Intel and AMD, giving Silicon Valley its name and its industry.

Gordon Moore & Moore's Law

Gordon Moore, co-founder of Intel, observed in 1965 that the number of transistors on a chip was roughly doubling about every year, later revised to about every two years.

  • Moore's Law became the industry's self-fulfilling roadmap for decades, driving exponential gains in computing power and drops in cost.
  • Physical limits (heat, quantum effects, atomic scale) have slowed the pace, pushing the industry toward multi-core designs, specialized accelerators (GPUs, TPUs), and advanced packaging.
⚠️ Not a law of physics:

Moore's Law is an economic and engineering observation, not a physical law. It held for roughly half a century because the industry organized itself around meeting it.

The Microprocessor — Intel 4004

The IC's ultimate expression was putting an entire CPU on one chip.

  • In 1971, Intel released the 4004, the first commercial microprocessor, designed by a team including Federico Faggin, Ted Hoff, and Masatoshi Shima.
  • It packed ~2,300 transistors and put the power of ENIAC onto a chip the size of a fingernail.
  • The microprocessor made the personal computer possible — a general-purpose CPU cheap enough for individuals.

Key Questions

Why did the transistor replace the vacuum tube?

Transistors are smaller, use far less power, generate less heat, switch faster, and are much more reliable and durable. Vacuum tubes burned out frequently and consumed enormous power. This shift made computers smaller, cheaper, and dependable enough for widespread use.

Kilby or Noyce — who invented the integrated circuit?

Both are credited. Kilby (TI) built the first working IC in 1958. Noyce (Fairchild) independently created a silicon IC using the planar process in 1959, which was practical to manufacture at scale. The industry generally recognizes them as co-inventors; Kilby received the Nobel Prize in 2000 (Noyce had died in 1990).

Is Moore's Law dead?

The classic transistor-doubling cadence has slowed as devices approach atomic scale and hit power/heat limits. Rather than "dead," the industry has shifted strategies: multi-core CPUs, GPUs and domain-specific accelerators, chiplets, and 3D stacking now deliver performance gains that single-die density alone no longer provides.
