John McCarthy — The Father of Artificial Intelligence

He gave a name to one of humanity's oldest dreams, invented a language so elegant it still feels modern, and imagined computing as a public utility decades before the cloud. Prickly, playful, and relentlessly logical, John McCarthy spent his life trying to teach machines to think.

A Radical Household and a Precocious Mind

John McCarthy was born in 1927 in Boston to politically radical immigrant parents — an Irish father and a Lithuanian-Jewish mother, both active in labor and communist politics. The family moved often, chasing work through the Depression, and settled in Los Angeles. John was a prodigy who taught himself college mathematics from textbooks while still in high school, and when he entered Caltech he was allowed to skip the first two years of math outright.

He earned his doctorate in mathematics at Princeton in 1951. It was there and in the years just after that a question seized him and never let go: could the precise machinery of logic and computation be used to capture something as slippery as thought itself? He had encountered the ideas of Alan Turing and John von Neumann, and he began to believe that intelligence might be something a machine could be programmed to possess.

1956 — Coining "Artificial Intelligence"

In 1955, while a young professor at Dartmouth, McCarthy and a few colleagues — including Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, and Nathaniel Rochester — proposed a summer research project. To describe this new field, McCarthy needed a name, and he deliberately coined a fresh one to set it apart from existing work: "artificial intelligence." He chose the phrase in part precisely because it was neutral and did not commit the field to any particular existing discipline.

The gathering that followed, the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, is now regarded as the founding event of the field. The proposal's famous conjecture — that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it" — set an audacious agenda that has driven AI research ever since. McCarthy had not merely named a discipline; he had helped call it into being.

🔑 Why the name mattered:

Naming the field "artificial intelligence" was more than a label. It staked out an ambition — to build machines that reason, not merely calculate — and it gathered a scattered set of researchers under one banner. Every debate we have today about what AI can and should do descends from the terms McCarthy set at Dartmouth in 1956.

Lisp — A Language Built on an Idea

To pursue AI, McCarthy needed a way to make computers manipulate not just numbers but symbols and ideas — lists, logical expressions, relationships. Around 1958 he designed a language to do exactly that: Lisp (from "LISt Processor"). It was the second-oldest high-level language still in use, appearing just after Backus's FORTRAN, and it was radically different in spirit.

Lisp was founded on beautiful and powerful ideas: that programs and data share the same structure (lists), so a Lisp program can read, generate, and transform other Lisp programs; that functions are first-class things that can be passed around like any other value; and that recursion — a function defined in terms of itself — is a natural way to express computation. It introduced or popularized concepts like automatic memory management ("garbage collection") and the read-evaluate-print loop. A famous piece of Lisp lore holds that McCarthy, to demonstrate the theory, defined the language's own evaluator in Lisp itself — a compact, self-describing kernel of astonishing elegance.

💡 The most influential language you may never have used:

Lisp's ideas — first-class functions, recursion, treating code as data, garbage collection, the interactive REPL — were decades ahead of their time and have steadily seeped into mainstream languages like Python, JavaScript, and beyond. Hackers have long revered it, and its dialects (Scheme, Common Lisp, Clojure) live on. Few languages have influenced so many others so deeply.

Time-Sharing and the Utility of Computing

In the late 1950s, computers were scarce, enormously expensive, and used one job at a time — you submitted your program on cards and waited hours or days for the result. McCarthy found this intolerable. He was among the earliest and most forceful advocates of time-sharing: the idea that a single powerful computer could serve many users at once, giving each the illusion of having the machine to themselves by rapidly switching its attention among them.

His advocacy helped launch influential time-sharing projects at MIT and elsewhere, transforming computing from a batch-processing ritual into something interactive and immediate. McCarthy went further still. As early as 1961, in a speech at MIT's centennial, he predicted that computing might one day be organized and sold as a public utility, like electricity or water — that people would draw computing power on demand from a shared network. It was, in essence, a vision of cloud computing, articulated half a century before it arrived.

⚠️ Ahead of the hardware:

Many of McCarthy's ideas outran the technology of his day. Time-sharing strained the machines of the 1960s; his grand ambitions for AI repeatedly ran into how genuinely hard the problem is. He was a visionary whose reach often exceeded the era's grasp — which is precisely why so many of his ideas look prophetic now.

The Stanford AI Lab

In 1962 McCarthy moved to Stanford University, where he founded the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (SAIL) and led it for decades. SAIL became one of the world's great crucibles of computer science, producing pioneering work in robotics, computer vision, speech, and the mathematical logic of common-sense reasoning — a problem McCarthy considered central and pursued to the end of his life.

He was fascinated by the question of how to give machines common sense: the vast, unspoken web of ordinary knowledge that humans use effortlessly. He invented formal tools such as circumscription to reason about what a system can assume when it lacks complete information. These were deep, difficult problems, and McCarthy pursued the logical, knowledge-based path to AI with conviction even as other approaches rose and fell around him.

A Mind That Would Not Sit Still

McCarthy was a memorable character: brilliant, blunt, argumentative, and often difficult, with a dry wit and a habit of long silences followed by sudden, penetrating remarks. He was endlessly curious about everything from science fiction to social policy, held strong and sometimes contrarian opinions, and maintained one of the early personal web pages, where he mused about human progress and sustainability. Colleagues learned that a question posed to McCarthy might be met with silence for a full minute — and then an answer worth the wait.

He received computing's highest honor, the Turing Award, in 1971, and his Turing lecture, "The Present State of Research on Artificial Intelligence," took stock of the field he had named. He remained active and opinionated into old age, still wrestling with the logic of common sense and the future of intelligent machines.

Legacy

John McCarthy died in 2011. His fingerprints are on an extraordinary range of modern computing. He named artificial intelligence and helped found it as a discipline. He created Lisp, whose ideas have quietly shaped nearly every language since. He championed time-sharing, which made computing interactive, and he foresaw computing-as-a-utility long before the cloud made it real.

Perhaps most of all, he insisted on a particular vision: that intelligence could be understood as something logical and formal, something that could be written down precisely enough for a machine to carry out. Whether or not that vision is fully vindicated, it set the terms of a quest that continues today, in every system that tries to reason, plan, or understand. The dream of the thinking machine, older than computing itself, owes its modern name and much of its language to him.

Did John McCarthy really coin the term "artificial intelligence"?

Yes. He coined the phrase in 1955 when writing the proposal for the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project, choosing a deliberately new and neutral term to define the emerging field. That workshop is regarded as the founding event of AI as a discipline.

What makes Lisp special?

Lisp, which McCarthy designed around 1958, was built on elegant ideas: programs and data share the same list structure (so code can manipulate code), functions are first-class values, and recursion is central. It pioneered concepts like garbage collection and the interactive REPL. Though rarely mainstream itself, its ideas have influenced nearly every modern language.

How did McCarthy anticipate cloud computing?

He was a leading advocate of time-sharing, letting many users share one computer interactively. In a 1961 speech he went further, predicting computing could one day be sold as a public utility like electricity — power drawn on demand from a shared network. That is essentially the vision of cloud computing, articulated decades early.
