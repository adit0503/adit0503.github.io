Konrad Zuse — The Computer Born in a Living Room

While teams in America and Britain worked with government money and giant labs, a lone German engineer built the world's first programmable, fully automatic computer on the floor of his parents' apartment — in a country at war, cut off from every other pioneer, using recycled telephone relays and film stock for punched tape. Then the bombs fell, and history nearly forgot him.

The "Lazy" Engineer

Konrad Zuse was born in Berlin on 22 June 1910 and grew up in a Germany between the wars. He trained as a civil engineer, and it was that training, oddly, that set him on the path to the computer. Structural engineering in the 1930s meant grinding through endless, repetitive calculations by hand — long tables of numbers that had to be computed and re-checked, hour after tedious hour.

Zuse, by his own cheerful admission, was lazy in the way that produces great inventions: he simply refused to keep doing dull work a machine ought to do. He quit his job at an aircraft factory to pursue, full-time and largely alone, a machine that could carry out any such calculation automatically. He had no formal training in mathematics or electronics, no team, and almost no money. What he had was a clear engineer's intuition about what such a machine needed to be.

The Z1 on the Living-Room Floor

From 1936 Zuse set up his workshop in his parents' Berlin apartment, and the living room slowly filled with metal. His first machine, later called the Z1, was completed around 1938 and was remarkable for two forward-looking choices. First, it worked in binary — ones and zeros — rather than the decimal system nearly everyone else was using. Second, it used floating-point arithmetic, letting it handle very large and very small numbers gracefully, a feature that would not become standard for years.

The Z1 was entirely mechanical, built from thousands of thin metal plates and sliding pins that Zuse and his friends cut out by hand with a jigsaw. It was programmable — instructions were fed in on punched tape, which Zuse improvised from discarded 35mm movie film, punching holes into the celluloid. It was a genuine marvel of design, but mechanically it was unreliable; the metal linkages jammed. The concept was sound, however, and Zuse knew what to change.

🔑 Why binary and floating-point were visionary:

Zuse chose binary because two-state switches are simple and reliable, the same insight that underlies all digital computing — the principle Claude Shannon proved for circuits at almost the same time. His use of floating-point representation, decades ahead of its general adoption, shows an engineer thinking clearly from first principles rather than copying what came before. There was no one to copy.

The Z3 — The First Working Computer

Replacing the balky mechanical logic with electromechanical telephone relays, Zuse built the Z2 as a proof of concept and then, in 1941, completed the Z3. This machine is widely regarded as the world's first working programmable, fully automatic digital computer. It used some 2,000 relays, worked in binary floating-point, was controlled by punched film tape, and could be programmed to carry out sequences of calculations without human intervention between steps.

The Z3 predated the American ENIAC by several years, and it was arguably more modern in conception — binary and floating-point where ENIAC was decimal, and controlled by a stored program on tape. Decades later it was even proven, in principle, to be Turing-complete (with a clever trick to handle the lack of conditional branching), meaning it could in theory compute anything any computer can. Zuse built all of this essentially by himself, in wartime isolation, unaware of the parallel work of Alan Turing, John von Neumann, or the American teams.

⚠️ Built for a regime, in the dark:

The uncomfortable truth is that Zuse's work took place in Nazi Germany, and some of his funding and applications were tied to the war effort, including calculations for aircraft and guided weapons. Zuse himself was not a party ideologue and worked in near-total secrecy; the German military never grasped the value of what he was doing and refused to fund his proposal for a faster electronic version. His machines were regarded as a private engineer's curiosity, not a strategic asset.

Destruction, Flight, and Survival

This is the tragedy at the heart of Zuse's story. In 1943 an Allied bombing raid destroyed the original Z1 and Z2, along with his workshop and much of his early documentation. The Z3 itself was destroyed in a bombing raid on Berlin in 1943. As the war closed in, Zuse managed to move his newer, larger machine — the Z4 — out of the collapsing city, transporting it by truck and hiding it in a barn in a small Alpine village in Bavaria as the Reich fell around him.

The Z4 survived, and in that sense so did Zuse's life's work, but the timing was cruel. By the time Germany recovered, the Americans and British had publicized their own machines, and Zuse's pioneering achievements — done first, done alone — were largely invisible to the wider world, buried in a defeated nation and lost documentation. He founded a company, Zuse KG, that built and sold computers commercially in the 1950s; the rebuilt Z4 went to work at ETH Zurich and became one of the first computers in regular scientific service in continental Europe.

Plankalkül — A Language Before Its Time

Isolated in the mountains with the war lost and little to do, Zuse turned to theory and, between roughly 1943 and 1945, designed what may be the world's first high-level programming language. He called it Plankalkül — "plan calculus." It included concepts strikingly ahead of its time: structured data types, arrays, conditional statements, loops, and even a notion of subroutines, all worked out on paper before any electronic computer existed to run them.

Plankalkül was so far ahead that it went almost entirely unpublished and unimplemented for decades; a working compiler for it was not built until the year 2000, long after Zuse's death. Had it been known in the 1940s, the history of programming languages might have run very differently. Instead it stands as one of the great "what ifs" of computing — a complete, sophisticated language conceived in an Alpine hideaway by a man cut off from the entire field he was helping to invent.

💡 An inventor and an artist:

In later life Zuse took up painting, sometimes signing his canvases "Kuno See," and produced portraits of computing colleagues and abstract works. He also proposed a philosophical idea he called a "Rechnender Raum" — a "computing space" — speculating that the universe itself might be the output of a giant cellular automaton, a computation. It was a startling notion from a practical engineer, and it anticipated ideas that serious physicists explore today.

The Pioneer History Nearly Lost

Konrad Zuse lived until 18 December 1995, long enough to be recognized, at last, as one of the true fathers of the computer. In his later decades he received honorary degrees, medals, and the belated respect of a field that had rediscovered him. He built faithful reconstructions of his early machines — a working replica of the Z3 he completed in 1961 still survives in a museum in Munich — determined that the record would not be lost a second time.

His story is a lesson in how history is written by circumstance as much as by merit. Zuse got there first, or very nearly, and did it with less support than any of his rivals — no Bletchley Park, no Manhattan Project budget, no university lab, just a living room, a jigsaw, some relays, and old movie film. That his name is less famous than others' is an accident of bombs and borders, not of achievement. When we say a computer is binary, programmable, and automatic, we are describing a machine a lonely German engineer built on his parents' floor while the world burned.

Was the Z3 really the first computer?

The Z3 (1941) is widely regarded as the first working programmable, fully automatic digital computer. It was binary, used floating-point arithmetic, and was controlled by punched tape — years before the American ENIAC. It was later shown to be Turing-complete in principle. "First computer" depends on definitions, but by the standard of a programmable automatic digital machine, the Z3 has a strong claim.

Why isn't Zuse more famous?

Timing and circumstance. He worked alone in wartime Nazi Germany, cut off from other pioneers, with minimal funding and no publicity. Allied bombing destroyed his early machines and much documentation, and by the time Germany recovered, the American and British machines had captured the world's attention. His achievements were real and often first, but they were largely invisible outside Germany for decades.

What was Plankalkül?

A high-level programming language Zuse designed on paper around 1943–45 while isolated in the Alps. It featured structured data types, arrays, loops, conditionals, and subroutines — concepts remarkably ahead of their time. It went largely unpublished, and no compiler for it was built until 2000. Had it been known in the 1940s, the history of programming languages might have unfolded very differently.
