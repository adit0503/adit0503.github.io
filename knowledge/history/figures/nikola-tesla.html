Nikola Tesla — The Man Who Electrified the World

He built the electrical foundation on which the entire information age would later rise — the alternating current that powers every data center and device — pioneered radio and wireless transmission, and dreamed of a world where energy and information flowed through the air. He died alone and near-penniless, in love with a pigeon, having imagined a future we are still catching up to.

Born in a Lightning Storm

Nikola Tesla was born, according to family legend, at the stroke of midnight during a fierce lightning storm on 10 July 1856, in the village of Smiljan in what is now Croatia, then part of the Austrian Empire. The midwife is said to have called the storm a bad omen, declaring the child would be a child of darkness; his mother replied that no, he would be a child of light. It is the kind of too-perfect story that clung to Tesla all his life — and electricity, the taming of the lightning, would indeed be his life's work.

He was a Serb by heritage, the son of an Orthodox priest, and possessed from childhood a mind of extraordinary and unsettling vividness. Tesla experienced intense flashes of light and could, he claimed, visualize machines in his imagination so completely that he could build, run, and even measure the wear on them entirely in his head before touching a tool. He also suffered severe compulsions — a horror of pearls and of touching hair, an obsession with the number three, a need to calculate the cubic volume of his meals — that would today be recognized as symptoms of obsessive-compulsive disorder.

The Vision in the Park

As a young engineer, Tesla became fixated on a problem his professors called impossible: the direct-current (DC) motors of the day were clumsy, spark-prone things, and he was convinced that alternating current (AC) could drive a far better motor with no troublesome commutator. The breakthrough came, by his own account, in a flash. Walking in a park in Budapest in 1882 and reciting poetry to a friend, the complete design of a rotating magnetic field — the heart of the AC induction motor — appeared to him whole. He sketched it in the dirt with a stick.

That idea would change the world. But first Tesla emigrated to the United States in 1884, arriving nearly penniless with a letter of introduction, and went to work for the most famous inventor on Earth: Thomas Edison.

The War of the Currents

The partnership with Edison soured quickly. Edison had staked his empire on direct current, which could not be transmitted efficiently over distance. Tesla's alternating current could — it could be stepped up to high voltage by a transformer, sent for miles along thin wires, then stepped back down. When Tesla left Edison and eventually partnered with the industrialist George Westinghouse, the stage was set for the bitter commercial and public-relations battle known as the War of the Currents.

Edison fought dirty, staging gruesome public electrocutions of animals to paint AC as a deadly menace and even lending his technology to the development of the electric chair, hoping the public would associate alternating current with death. It failed. AC's technical superiority was overwhelming. The decisive victories came when Westinghouse and Tesla lit the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago with AC, dazzling millions, and then built the great hydroelectric power plant at Niagara Falls, which sent AC power to the city of Buffalo. The world would run on alternating current — Tesla's current — and it still does.

🔑 Why this is the ground the digital world stands on:

Every server in every data center, every phone charger, every fiber-optic switch, draws its power from an alternating-current grid descended directly from Tesla and Westinghouse's system. The information age is built on cheap, transmissible electric power, and it was Tesla's polyphase AC — the motor, the transformer, the generator — that made continent-spanning electrification possible. Before information could flow, power had to. Tesla built the power.

The Showman of the Electrical Age

Tesla was not only an inventor but a magnificent showman, and he understood spectacle as few scientists ever have. In his lectures he would pass hundreds of thousands of volts through his own body to light lamps held in his hands, or let long streamers of artificial lightning crackle around him while he stood, unharmed and theatrical, in the glare. Audiences left convinced they had glimpsed a wizard.

At his laboratory in Colorado Springs around 1899, he built enormous Tesla coils capable of throwing artificial lightning bolts many meters long, with thunderclaps heard miles away. He reported drawing so much power that he burned out the local generator. He claimed to have detected mysterious repeating signals he wondered might come from another planet — an early, romantic brush with the idea of receiving intelligence across space. Whether cosmic noise or a distant experiment, the story captures the man: forever reaching past the edge of the known.

⚠️ Genius entangled with myth:

Tesla's reputation today swings between neglect and near-worship, and the truth is muddied by legend. Some claims made in his name — wireless energy for the whole planet, a "death ray," earthquake machines — were real proposals or experiments, but often unproven, exaggerated, or never built. He was a genuine and monumental inventor, but not a magician; separating his enormous real achievements from the mythology that surrounds him is part of understanding him honestly.

Radio, Wireless, and Wardenclyffe

Tesla's deepest obsession was the wireless transmission of both information and power. He demonstrated radio-controlled devices in the 1890s — famously guiding a small boat by remote control on a pool in Madison Square Garden in 1898, astonishing a crowd who thought it magic or mind-reading. He held fundamental patents in radio, and after a long legal saga the U.S. Supreme Court in 1943 upheld key Tesla patents over those of Guglielmo Marconi, affirming his foundational role in radio's invention (though Marconi built the first practical transatlantic systems).

His grandest dream was Wardenclyffe, a great tower on Long Island from which he intended to transmit not just messages but usable electrical power wirelessly across the globe — free energy through the air, drawn down anywhere on Earth. It was a breathtaking, arguably impossible vision, and it was his ruin. His backer, the financier J. P. Morgan, withdrew support — the story goes that Morgan saw no way to meter and charge for energy given freely to all. The tower was never completed and was eventually demolished for scrap. Tesla's most ambitious dream collapsed into debt.

Pigeons, Poverty, and Prophecy

Tesla's later decades were a long, poignant decline. Never good with money and uninterested in it, he drifted from grand hotel to grand hotel, running up debts, giving eccentric birthday-press conferences full of astonishing (and unverifiable) claims. He never married and lived largely alone. His great companions became the pigeons of New York, which he fed devotedly in the parks and even in his rooms. He spoke of one particular white pigeon that he loved, he said, "as a man loves a woman," and grieved when it died as though he had lost a person.

He died alone in a room at the Hotel New Yorker on 7 January 1943, at eighty-six, deeply in debt and largely forgotten by the public that had once thrilled to his lightning. His papers were seized by the U.S. government, which feared his notes on a rumored "death ray" or particle-beam weapon might fall into enemy hands during the war — a final flourish of the mystery that always trailed him.

💡 A prophet of the wireless world:

In a 1926 interview Tesla described, with uncanny accuracy, a future in which people would carry small instruments in their pockets, communicate instantly across the world by image and voice, and see and hear one another as if in the same room. He was, in effect, describing the smartphone and the wireless internet, decades before either was remotely possible. He saw the shape of our connected age long before the technology to build it existed.

Why Tesla Belongs to the Information Age

Tesla did not build a computer, and he stands a little apart from the logicians and engineers elsewhere in these pages. Yet he belongs firmly in this story, because the electrical and the informational ages are one continuous arc. The digital revolution is, at bottom, the disciplined control of electricity — and it was Tesla, more than almost anyone, who taught humanity to generate, transmit, and harness electric power at scale. His alternating current is the literal lifeblood of every machine that computes.

His vision reached even further: toward a world of information and energy carried invisibly through the air, of instant global communication, of machines controlled from a distance. Wi-Fi, mobile networks, wireless charging, remote-piloted craft — these are, in spirit, Tesla's dreams made real. He was granted his measure of immortality at last: the unit of magnetic flux density is the tesla, and his name now rides on the world's most famous electric cars. The child of light, born in the storm, turned out to be a prophet of the age we live in — an age that runs on his current and reaches, still, toward his dreams.

1888  Patents the AC induction motor; joins Westinghouse
1893  AC lights the Chicago World's Fair
1895  Niagara Falls hydroelectric plant delivers AC power

1943  Dies penniless in New York; his radio patents upheld
What is Tesla's single most important contribution?

His polyphase alternating-current (AC) system — the induction motor, transformer, and generator — which made it possible to transmit electric power efficiently over long distances. This won the "War of the Currents" against Edison's DC and became the basis of the electrical grid worldwide. Every device and data center in the modern world draws power from a grid descended from Tesla's design.

Did Tesla invent radio?

He held fundamental radio patents and demonstrated wireless control in the 1890s, and in 1943 the U.S. Supreme Court upheld key Tesla patents over Marconi's. However, Marconi built the first practical long-distance radio systems. It's fairest to say Tesla was one of radio's essential foundational inventors, even though he isn't the sole "inventor" of it.

Why does an electrical inventor belong in a computing history?

Because the information age is built on controlled electricity. Before data could flow, cheap and transmissible electric power had to exist, and Tesla's AC system provided exactly that. His further dreams of wireless power and instant global communication anticipated Wi-Fi, mobile networks, and the connected world — making him a genuine forerunner of the age of information, not just electricity.
