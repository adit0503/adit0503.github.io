Alan Turing — The Man Who Imagined the Machine

He defined what a computer is before one existed, broke the code that helped win a world war, asked whether machines could think, and was destroyed by the nation he had saved for the crime of being who he was. No single life sits closer to the heart of this story.

The Boy Who Ran to School

Alan Mathison Turing was born in London on 23 June 1912, into a respectable colonial-service family that largely left him and his brother in the care of others while his parents served in India. He was a solitary, untidy, brilliant boy, hopeless at the things Edwardian schools prized — neat handwriting, classical polish, team spirit — and hungry for the things they did not, chemistry and mathematics above all.

An early anecdote captures the stubbornness that would define him. When he started at his boarding school in 1926, a national transport strike shut down the trains. Rather than miss the first day, the thirteen-year-old Turing simply cycled sixty miles from Southampton to Sherborne, stopping overnight at an inn along the way. It made the local newspaper. He would spend his life traveling routes no one else thought worth attempting.

Christopher Morcom — Loss and Direction

At Sherborne, Turing formed an intense attachment to a fellow pupil named Christopher Morcom — a gifted, popular boy who returned Turing's friendship and shared his passion for science. It was, on Turing's side, almost certainly his first love. In 1930, with shattering suddenness, Morcom died of bovine tuberculosis.

The loss reshaped Turing. Grieving, he turned over questions of mind, matter, and spirit — whether Morcom's mind could somehow persist beyond his body — and these questions, half mystical at first, hardened over the years into a rigorous fascination with the relationship between the physical brain and the immaterial mind. What is thought, physically? Could it exist in something other than flesh? The boy's grief became, in time, one of the deepest questions of computer science.

🔑 A mind drawn to foundations:

Turing was never content to use a tool without asking what made it work at the deepest level. At Cambridge, where he studied mathematics from 1931, he was drawn to the great crisis of the age — the effort by logicians to nail down exactly what mathematics could and could not prove. That question would hand him the key to the computer.

1936 — The Machine on Paper

In 1936, at just twenty-three, Turing wrote a paper with a forbidding title — On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem — that quietly founded computer science. The German phrase refers to a challenge posed by the mathematician David Hilbert: was there a mechanical procedure that could decide, for any mathematical statement, whether it was provable?

To answer it, Turing had to define, with total precision, what a "mechanical procedure" even was. He imagined an abstract device — now called a Turing machine — of almost childlike simplicity: an infinite paper tape divided into squares, a head that could read and write symbols one square at a time, and a small table of rules telling it what to do. That is all. And yet he proved that such a machine, given the right rules, could carry out any computation that any machine could ever perform. He then described a universal machine that could imitate any other by reading its description from the tape — the theoretical blueprint of the general-purpose computer, and of the stored program.

He also proved something humbling: that some problems are undecidable — no machine, however powerful, can solve them. He had drawn the map of what computation can and cannot do, and he had done it before a single electronic computer existed. Every device we call a computer today is, formally, a Turing machine.

⚠️ Two men, one idea:

The American logician Alonzo Church reached an equivalent result at almost the same moment using different methods. The combined insight is now called the Church–Turing thesis. Turing went to Princeton to study under Church, earning his PhD in 1938 — and turned down a job in America to return home to England, just as war was gathering.

Bletchley Park and the War of Codes

When war broke out in 1939, Turing reported to Bletchley Park, the secret home of Britain's codebreakers. The central problem was the German Enigma machine, which scrambled military orders with a cipher that changed every day and had, on paper, more possible settings than there are atoms in a small galaxy. Cracking it by hand each day was hopeless.

Building on earlier work by Polish cryptanalysts, Turing designed an electromechanical machine called the Bombe, which could rifle through vast numbers of Enigma settings and eliminate the impossible ones at speed, exploiting logical weaknesses and the small, human predictabilities of German operators — a weather report at the same time each morning, a predictable sign-off. Once broken, the daily traffic of the German military lay open to Allied eyes.

Historians estimate that the intelligence flowing from Bletchley — code-named Ultra — shortened the war by perhaps two years and saved many millions of lives, particularly in the Battle of the Atlantic, where reading U-boat orders let convoys steer clear of wolfpacks. Turing was central to it. He was also, by every account, gloriously eccentric: he chained his tea mug to a radiator to stop it being stolen, cycled to work in a gas mask to ward off hay fever, and — genuinely fearing invasion and currency collapse — converted his savings into silver ingots and buried them in a wood, then lost the coded map to their location and never found them again.

💡 A secret kept for decades:

Bletchley's work was so secret that it remained classified for thirty years. Turing and his colleagues could tell no one — not family, not friends — what they had done. When Turing died, the public had no idea he had helped win the war. His full role only emerged in the 1970s and after, long past any chance of thanking him.

Can Machines Think?

After the war Turing helped design some of the first true stored-program computers, first at the National Physical Laboratory and then at Manchester. But his restless mind returned to the old question that had haunted him since Christopher Morcom's death: the relationship between machine and mind.

In 1950 he published Computing Machinery and Intelligence, opening with the blunt question, "Can machines think?" Finding the question too vague, he replaced it with a practical test — the Imitation Game, now universally called the Turing Test: if a human interrogator, exchanging typed messages, cannot reliably tell a machine from a person, on what grounds can we deny the machine intelligence? In the same paper he carefully addressed the objections, including "Lady Lovelace's Objection" — Ada Lovelace's century-old argument that a machine can only do what it is told. With that paper Turing effectively founded the field of artificial intelligence, and set the terms of a debate we are still having every time we talk to a chatbot today.

Persecution and a Poisoned Apple

Here the story turns to tragedy of a peculiarly cruel kind. In 1952, Turing reported a burglary at his home. In the course of the investigation he spoke openly to the police about a relationship with another man — and in the Britain of that era, homosexual acts were a crime. Turing was charged with "gross indecency," the same statute under which Oscar Wilde had been imprisoned half a century before.

He did not hide or deny it. Convicted, he was given a choice between prison and chemical castration — a course of hormone injections meant to suppress his sex drive. He chose the injections so he could keep working. The treatment caused physical changes and, by many accounts, deep distress. His security clearance was revoked; the man who had helped break Enigma was now considered a security risk and shut out of the secret work he had led.

On 7 June 1954, Alan Turing was found dead at home. Beside him lay a partly eaten apple, and the cause of death was cyanide poisoning. The inquest ruled suicide. It is widely believed he laced the apple himself — a grim echo, some have speculated, of the poisoned apple in Snow White, a film he loved. He was forty-one years old. (Some, including his mother, argued it may have been an accident from his home chemistry experiments; the truth cannot now be known.)

The Apology, Half a Century Late

For decades Turing's memory carried the double shadow of official secrecy and social shame. Only slowly did the world learn what he had done — at Bletchley, in mathematics, in the founding of AI — and reckon with how he had been treated.

In 2009, after a public campaign, the British Prime Minister issued a formal apology for the "appalling" way Turing had been treated. In 2013 he was granted a royal pardon. And in 2017 the "Alan Turing law" retroactively pardoned tens of thousands of men convicted under the same cruel statutes. In 2021 his face was placed on the £50 note — the highest-value note in circulation — so that the man once branded a criminal now looks out from the currency of the country that once condemned him.

It is impossible to read Turing's life without a sense of loss — for the years the persecution stole, for the work he never lived to do. But the shape of his gift is clear. He told us, with mathematical certainty, what a computer is and what it can never do. He helped save the free world and was sworn to never say so. And he asked, first and most seriously, whether the machines he had imagined might one day think. We live inside the answers he began.

What is a Turing machine and why does it matter?

It's an abstract device — an infinite tape, a read/write head, and a table of rules — that Turing defined in 1936 to formalize what "computation" means. He proved a single universal machine could simulate any other, which is the theoretical basis of the general-purpose, stored-program computer. Anything a modern computer can compute, a Turing machine can too; it defines the limits of computability itself.

What did Turing actually do at Bletchley Park?

He led work on breaking the German Enigma cipher, designing the electromechanical Bombe that rapidly eliminated impossible cipher settings to find each day's key. The resulting intelligence (Ultra) is widely credited with shortening World War II by around two years and saving millions of lives. The work stayed secret for decades.

What is the Turing Test?

Proposed in his 1950 paper, it sidesteps the vague question "can machines think?" with a practical one: if a human judge exchanging typed messages cannot reliably distinguish a machine from a person, the machine should be considered intelligent. It effectively founded the field of artificial intelligence and still frames debates about machine intelligence today.
