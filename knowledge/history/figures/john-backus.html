John Backus — The Man Who Freed the Programmer

A self-described aimless young man who nearly flunked out of everything found his calling wrestling with a room-sized IBM machine — and then led a small team that dared to believe a program could write its own machine code as well as any human. FORTRAN proved them right and changed programming forever.

A Drifter Finds a Machine

John Warner Backus was born in 1924 in Philadelphia and raised in comfortable circumstances in Wilmington, Delaware. By his own cheerful admission, he was a poor and unmotivated student who drifted through school, flunked out of one course of study after another, and had little idea what to do with himself. After wartime Army service, he wandered into a radio-technician course, discovered he had a real gift for mathematics, and eventually earned a master's degree in the subject from Columbia University in 1949.

The turning point was almost casual. Touring IBM's Manhattan headquarters, he saw the giant Selective Sequence Electronic Calculator and mentioned to a guide that he was looking for work. On the spot he was steered upstairs for an interview and hired as a programmer. The aimless young man had found the machine that would give his life its direction.

The Misery of Programming by Hand

Programming in the early 1950s was brutal, arcane labor. To make a computer do anything, you wrote in its raw machine code or in barely-more-friendly assembly language — long strings of numeric instructions and memory addresses, every one hand-crafted and easy to get wrong. A single misplaced number could send a program careening off into nonsense, and finding the error might take days.

Backus knew this misery intimately; he had written an early assembly aid called Speedcoding for one of IBM's machines. He grasped a crucial economic truth that others overlooked: the cost of programming and debugging was becoming as large as the cost of the expensive machines themselves. If programmers could express their intentions in something closer to ordinary mathematics, and let the computer translate it into machine code, the savings would be enormous.

🔑 The core idea of a high-level language:

Let the human write X = A + B * C — clear algebra — and have a program called a compiler automatically turn that into the dozens of low-level instructions the machine actually executes. The programmer thinks about the problem; the machine handles the tedium. It sounds obvious now. In 1953 it sounded to many like a fantasy.

FORTRAN — The Impossible Bet

In 1953 Backus proposed to IBM that he lead a project to build such a system for the new IBM 704. The result, delivered in 1957, was FORTRAN — from "Formula Translation" — the first widely successful high-level programming language. His small, brilliant, and famously informal team labored for three years to build the compiler that would translate FORTRAN's algebra-like statements into efficient machine code.

The whole enterprise rested on a wager that many thought reckless. Skeptics insisted that no automatic translator could ever produce code as tight and fast as what a skilled programmer wrote by hand — and on the costly machines of the day, speed was everything. Backus's team essentially bet their project that a compiler could rival hand-written assembly. To have any hope, they poured extraordinary effort into optimization, inventing techniques that would let the compiler generate remarkably efficient code.

Winning Over the Skeptics

They won the bet. When FORTRAN shipped, its compiled programs ran nearly as fast as expertly hand-coded ones — while taking a fraction of the time to write. Scientists and engineers, who cared about equations and not about memory addresses, embraced it eagerly. A calculation that once took weeks to program could now be written in an afternoon.

The impact was profound and lasting. FORTRAN proved that high-level languages were not merely convenient toys but practical, efficient tools — a demonstration that opened the floodgates for every language that followed. It became the dominant language of scientific and engineering computing for decades, and in modernized forms it is still used today in weather modeling, physics, and high-performance computing. Backus had not just built a language; he had established that the compiler was one of the central ideas of computer science.

💡 Why FORTRAN's success mattered so much:

Had FORTRAN been slow, the skeptics would have been vindicated and high-level programming might have stalled for years. Because it was fast and easy, it settled the argument for good. Every language you have ever used — from C to Python — descends from the door FORTRAN kicked open in 1957.

Backus–Naur Form

Backus's second great contribution came at the end of the 1950s, during the international effort to design the language ALGOL. To describe a programming language precisely, one needs a rigorous way to write down its grammar — exactly which sequences of symbols form valid statements. Backus devised such a notation, which the Danish computer scientist Peter Naur refined and used in the ALGOL report.

The result is called Backus–Naur Form, or BNF. It is a compact, formal way of specifying the syntax of a language — a set of rules describing how valid programs are constructed. BNF became one of the fundamental tools of computer science: virtually every programming language since has had its grammar described in BNF or a close variant, and it sits at the heart of how compilers are built. It was a fitting echo of Backus's gift for turning messy human intention into precise, mechanical form.

⚠️ A shared credit, honestly noted:

The notation is named for both Backus and Naur, and there was some later discussion about how much each contributed and whose earlier ideas (including those of Turing's contemporaries in logic) it built upon. The double name reflects a genuine collaboration; Backus himself was generous in acknowledging Naur's role in shaping it into the form the world adopted.

A Restless Critic of His Own Work

What makes Backus unusual among pioneers is that he grew dissatisfied with the very style of programming he had done so much to create. When he received the prestigious Turing Award in 1977, he used his acceptance lecture not to celebrate FORTRAN but to critique it — arguing that conventional languages, FORTRAN included, had grown bloated and clumsy, chaining programmers to the step-by-step "word-at-a-time" thinking of the underlying hardware.

He spent his later career championing an alternative he called function-level or functional programming, in which programs are built by composing functions rather than issuing sequences of commands. His system, FP, never caught on directly, but the ideas he pressed helped nourish the functional-programming movement that has since flowed into mainstream languages. It was a remarkable act of intellectual honesty: the father of the imperative high-level language spent his last decades arguing that the field had taken a wrong turn.

Legacy

John Backus died in 2007. His legacy rests on two foundations that hold up much of modern computing. FORTRAN proved that programmers could be freed from machine code without sacrificing performance, launching the entire enterprise of high-level languages and compilers. Backus–Naur Form gave computer scientists a precise language for describing languages, a tool still taught and used everywhere.

Alongside contemporaries like John McCarthy, who was building Lisp in the same years, Backus helped transform programming from an arcane craft practiced by a priesthood of specialists into something scientists, engineers, and eventually millions of people could do. The drifting student who wandered into IBM ended up handing the world the keys to the machine.

What was FORTRAN and why did it matter?

FORTRAN (Formula Translation), released by John Backus's IBM team in 1957, was the first widely successful high-level programming language. It let programmers write in an algebra-like notation that a compiler translated into efficient machine code. Its success proved high-level languages could be both easy and fast, launching every programming language that followed.

What is Backus–Naur Form?

BNF is a formal notation for describing the grammar (syntax) of a programming language — the rules for which sequences of symbols form valid statements. Devised by Backus and refined by Peter Naur for the ALGOL language, it became a fundamental tool of computer science and is used to specify the syntax of nearly every language.

Why did Backus criticize his own creation?

In his 1977 Turing Award lecture, Backus argued that conventional languages like FORTRAN forced programmers into rigid, step-by-step thinking tied to the hardware. He advocated functional programming, where programs are built by composing functions. His system FP never became popular, but the ideas influenced later functional languages.
